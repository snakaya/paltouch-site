What to Buy in Japan: Konbini, 100-Yen Shops and Drugstores

Written in Japan. Everything here is something you can walk in and buy today.

You do not need a special souvenir shop. Three ordinary stores cover almost everything worth bringing home, and locals use all three every week.

Konbini: the 15-minute stop

Convenience stores are not an emergency option here. They are where people buy lunch, socks, concert tickets and gifts.

  • Regional KitKat and chocolate. Flavors change by season and by region, and the boxes are made to be handed out at an office.
  • Senbei and rice crackers. Flat, light, and they survive a suitcase.
  • Instant coffee and tea bags. A small jar of instant coffee is a normal gift here, and it is one of the few grocery items that travels safely.
  • Sweets you cannot get abroad, like soft candy, mochi-style chews and seasonal puddings. Eat the fresh ones before you fly.

Skip the cup noodles, retort curry and anything with a meat broth. See the customs section below for why.

100-yen shops: the best value in the country

Daiso, Seria and Can Do sell most items for 110 yen including tax, which is roughly 70 US cents at recent rates. The quality surprises people.

  • Kitchen tools: fine graters, sesame grinders, bento dividers, silicone lids.
  • Stationery: sticky notes, pens, sticker sheets, folders.
  • Chopsticks and small tableware, including the small dishes that cost ten times as much abroad.
  • Beauty tools: blotting paper, brushes, eyelash curlers, nail files.
  • Storage: flat, foldable boxes that fill the gaps in a suitcase.

Drugstores: cosmetics and skincare

Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Sundrug and Welcia are on every shopping street. This is where most of the beauty items people ask about actually come from.

  • Cleansing oil and balm, in sizes that make sense to carry.
  • Sheet masks, sold in packs of five or thirty.
  • Hand cream, lip balm and hair treatments.
  • Sunscreen, which is the one category to read carefully. The US regulates sunscreen as a drug and has approved a different list of UV filters than Japan, and the UK, Canada and Australia each run their own approval list too, so a Japanese sunscreen is not automatically a better or safer choice for you. FDA: Cosmetics importers

We do not cover Japanese medicines here. Painkillers, eye drops and stomach remedies sold in Japanese drugstores can contain ingredients that are not approved where you live, so this site does not recommend buying them to take or ship home.

What you cannot bring home

This is the part most shopping guides skip, and it is the part that gets suitcases opened at the airport. The rules are not the same everywhere, so the honest answer depends on where you are flying back to.

Safe almost everywhere: candy, chocolate, biscuits, senbei and rice crackers, packaged tea and coffee, and sealed seasonings that contain no meat.

Not worth packing anywhere: anything containing meat, fresh fruit and vegetables, plants and seeds, and fresh sweets that will not survive the flight.

Canada Every food item has to be declared, and most categories are capped at 20 kg or 20 L per person, including confectionery, snacks, tea and coffee. Eggs and egg products are not permitted from Japan. Only commercially prepared, cooked, shelf-stable meat in sealed containers is allowed at all, and further country restrictions sit on top of that, so meat is not worth the gamble. Australia The strictest of the four. Meat is prohibited in every form, fresh, dried, preserved or cured, and whole eggs and egg products are prohibited too. Commercially packaged noodles and pasta made only from plant ingredients are permitted. Seeds need a phytosanitary certificate. Everything goes on the Incoming Passenger Card. Declare everything, wherever you land. If you declare food and it turns out you cannot bring it in, it is taken away and that is the end of it. If you do not declare it, you can be fined: Australia can issue an infringement notice of up to A$7,280 and cancel your visa, and explicitly says you will not be penalised if you declare and present the goods. Practical notes

  • Cash still matters in small shops, although cards and IC cards work almost everywhere in cities. Foreign cards are accepted in chains but not always in small local shops.
  • Sizes run small. A Japanese L is often closer to a US or UK small to medium, and shoes are sold in centimetres rather than US, UK or AU sizes.
  • Many larger stores offer tax-free shopping for visitors with a passport, with a minimum spend. The rules change, so ask at the register before you pay.
  • Buy the flat things. Suitcase space runs out faster than money does.
