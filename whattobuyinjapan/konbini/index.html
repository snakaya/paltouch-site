CONVENIENCE STORES

Konbini Shopping in Japan: Snacks, Prices and What Survives the Flight

Written in Japan. Prices are typical shelf prices including tax, and they move, so treat them as a guide rather than a quote.

Disclosure: The links on this page go straight to each company and we are not paid for them. If that ever changes, this notice will say so and the paid links will be marked.

A Japanese convenience store is not a last resort. People buy lunch, socks, concert tickets and gifts in them, and there are roughly 56,000 of them, so you are never far from one. For a visitor they are the cheapest and least intimidating place to start shopping.

Throughout this page, yen figures are converted at roughly 150 yen to the US dollar. Check the rate on the day.

7-Eleven, FamilyMart and Lawson carry similar things, but each has a category it does better. If you only walk into one, this is how to choose.

What a 500-yen konbini breakfast looks like

Around 500 yen, about $3.40, buys a real breakfast. Four combinations that work, built from what is actually on the morning shelf:

  • Two rice balls and a bottled tea. Roughly 150–250 yen each for the onigiri, 130–180 for the drink. Salmon and kelp are the safe first choices.
  • An egg sandwich and a coffee. The egg sandwich is a national institution and costs 250–350 yen. Machine coffee is 120–200 yen.
  • A pastry, a banana and a yogurt drink. Around 150, 120 and 150 yen. The lightest option if you are heading straight out.
  • A bowl of miso soup and a rice ball. Instant miso cups are 120–200 yen and most stores have hot water at the counter.

Pacing your food budget this way matters more than it sounds on a long trip. Two konbini breakfasts cost less than one hotel breakfast.

What survives a 12-hour flight, and what melts

Chocolate behaves differently in a checked bag than in a carry-on. The hold is cold in the air but your bag can sit on hot tarmac at both ends, and a carry-on rides in a heated cabin.

  • Holds up well: senbei, hard candy, biscuit sticks in their box, wafer sweets, anything dry and individually wrapped.
  • Risky in summer: plain chocolate bars and chocolate-coated biscuits. Pack them in the middle of clothing in the checked bag, not in a carry-on that sits in the sun on a shuttle bus.
  • Do not try: chilled desserts, fresh mochi, cream-filled sweets and anything with a use-by date measured in days. These also risk being refused at the border as perishable.

Candy and chocolate themselves are generally fine to declare and carry, which is why they dominate this page.

Six seasonings that travel home easily

Sealed, shelf-stable and flat. These are the items that turn a souvenir into something you actually use.

  • Furikake — dry rice seasoning in sachets or a shaker. Check the label: some contain meat.
  • Shichimi togarashi — seven-spice chilli blend in a small cylinder. Weighs almost nothing.
  • Sesame seeds, toasted or ground — sold in flat sealed packs.
  • Yuzu kosho — citrus and chilli paste. A small tub, but it is a paste, so checked bag only.
  • Matcha powder in a tin — sealed, light, and allowed everywhere covered below.
  • Curry roux blocks — read the box. Many contain beef or pork and are not worth the risk.

Skip the soup stock. Dashi packs and bouillon cubes very often contain meat extract, and they are the single most common thing confiscated from a shopping haul.

What you cannot bring home

This is the part most shopping guides skip, and it is the part that gets suitcases opened at the airport. The rules are not the same everywhere, so the honest answer depends on where you are flying back to.

Safe almost everywhere: candy, chocolate, biscuits, senbei and rice crackers, packaged tea and coffee, and sealed seasonings that contain no meat.

Not worth packing anywhere: anything containing meat, fresh fruit and vegetables, plants and seeds, and fresh sweets that will not survive the flight.

Australia The strictest of the four. Meat is prohibited in every form, fresh, dried, preserved or cured, and whole eggs and egg products are prohibited too.
